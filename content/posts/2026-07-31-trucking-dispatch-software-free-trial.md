Most dispatch software trials get wasted. Somebody signs up on a Tuesday afternoon, clicks around the dashboard for eleven minutes, gets pulled into a check call, and never opens the tab again. Thirty days later the trial expires and the only thing anyone learned is what the login screen looks like.

That's not a knock on the person doing the evaluating. Dispatchers and ops managers at 10-75 truck operations don't have spare hours to "explore a platform." You're covering loads, chasing PODs, and fielding the driver who's sitting at a shipper with a detention clock running. A trial has to prove itself fast, in the middle of real work, or it's not worth the login.

So here's a checklist. Ten things to actually do during a trial period, not just look at. Run these against any dispatch software you're evaluating, whether that's Techvia TMS or something else, and you'll know inside a week whether the tool fits your operation or just has a nice landing page.

## 1. Build one full RateCon-to-invoice cycle

Take a real load — an old one you've already delivered — and rebuild it start to finish inside the trial account. Enter the RateCon details, assign a truck and driver, run it through dispatch, mark it delivered, attach the POD, and generate the customer invoice. If any step forces you to jump into a spreadsheet or a separate app to finish the job, that's a gap you'll hit weekly once you're live.

## 2. Drag a load around the board and see what breaks

A drag-and-drop dispatch board sounds simple until you're mid-shift and need to bump a load from one driver to another because the first truck blew a tire outside Amarillo. Grab a load on the board, move it to a different truck, change the pickup window, and confirm the driver-facing view updates. If the reassignment takes four clicks and a page reload, count that against it.

## 3. Add a driver who has zero interest in learning new software

Every fleet has one. Pick that driver — mentally, at least — and picture them getting a tracking link on their phone. Test the driver-facing side yourself: send a magic link, see if it opens a GPS view without an app download or a password. If your drivers need training to check in, the software has already lost half the fleet.

## 4. Run a load with a GPS-tracked truck, not a hypothetical one

If the platform integrates with Samsara ELD or offers driver magic links for live tracking, don't take the sales page's word for it. Assign an actual truck with a live ELD feed, or send yourself a tracking link like a driver would, and watch the position update in real time. This is the difference between a dispatcher making a proactive check call and a dispatcher finding out about a delay when the customer calls angry.

## 5. Process a driver settlement and a carrier settlement

Settlements are where dispatch software either saves you a Friday afternoon or adds one. Pull a completed load through to a driver settlement — pay type, deductions, whatever your structure is — and separately run a carrier settlement if you broker any freight out. Check the math. Check whether it exports cleanly for your accountant. A tool that handles dispatch beautifully but chokes on settlements is only solving half your week.

## 6. Send and receive an EDI 204 load tender

If you're running freight for shippers or brokers who tender electronically, this one matters more than almost anything else on the list. Test whether the system can actually receive an EDI X12 204 tender and turn it into a workable load without someone re-keying it by hand. A lot of tools claim EDI support and mean "we can export a CSV." That's not the same thing.

## 7. Log a detention claim from pickup to resolution

Pick a load, note a two-hour wait at the shipper, and see how the software lets you document it. Where does the detention time get recorded — is it tied to the load, timestamped, and easy to reference when you're invoicing the broker three weeks later? Detention disputes get won or lost on documentation, and if your trial software makes that annoying, your real accounts receivable will feel it.

## 8. Ask the AI Dispatcher to rank a stack of loads

If the platform has a feature that ranks loads by margin and deadhead — like Techvia TMS's AI Dispatcher — feed it a handful of real load options from a recent week, ones you already know the outcome of. See if its ranking matches what you'd have picked with your own gut and a calculator. You're not testing whether it's smarter than you; you're testing whether it saves you the ten minutes of mental math you do fifteen times a day.

## 9. Pull a compliance report cold

Don't ask for a demo of the compliance tracking. Go into the account yourself and try to find out which driver's medical card expires next month, or which truck is due for its next inspection window. If you can't find that in under a minute without help, your safety manager won't use it either.

## 10. Add every user your team actually has

This is the one people skip, and it's the one that changes the math the most. Add your dispatcher, your safety person, your billing clerk, your owner-operator liaison — everyone who'd touch the system day to day. Some platforms charge per seat, which means the price you saw on the homepage isn't the price you'll pay once your whole team is in. Techvia TMS runs flat at $199 a month for unlimited users, so a 10-truck shop and a 75-truck shop pay the same rate whether they've got three logins or fifteen. Test that assumption against whatever you're evaluating — add real headcount and see what the invoice actually says.

### Why the ten days matter more than the thirty

A 30-day trial gives you room, but the real signal shows up in the first week if you're testing with live loads instead of dummy data. Dummy data never argues back. A real RateCon with a weird accessorial, a real driver who fat-fingers a check call, a real detention dispute — those tell you things a guided demo never will.
