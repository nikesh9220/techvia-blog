Buying a TMS is a lot like buying a used truck. The paint job tells you nothing about what's happening under the hood, and the salesperson's job is to keep your eyes on the paint. We've sat through enough demos, on both sides of the table, to know that most TMS evaluations fail for the same reason: brokers and carriers shop features when they should be shopping honesty.

This is a checklist, not a pitch. Use it against any vendor you're looking at, including us.

## Start With Your Operation, Not Their Feature List

Before you take a single demo call, write down what actually happens in your business on a Tuesday. How many loads move through dispatch? How many drivers are you settling out each week? Do you run EDI tenders from a handful of big shippers, or is everything phone and email? Do you carry your own trucks, broker out the overflow, or both?

A TMS evaluation that starts with "show me your dispatch board" instead of "here's how we run freight" is backwards. The vendor should be asking you questions, not the other way around, for at least the first ten minutes.

### Questions To Ask Before You Ask About Price

Ask these before pricing ever comes up, because the answers tell you whether the tool fits your operation or whether you'll be working around it in six months:

- How does a load move from quote to invoice to settlement inside the system, step by step?
- What happens when a driver's ELD drops off Samsara mid-route — does dispatch lose visibility, or does the system flag it?
- Can I see detention time and deadhead miles on the same screen I use to check load margin?
- If a carrier's insurance lapses, does the system stop me from dispatching to them, or is that on me to catch manually?
- How do RateCons and PODs get attached to a load file, and who can see them?

If the sales rep fumbles those, that's your answer. Not because they don't know the software — because the software probably wasn't built by people who've done a check call at 6 a.m. asking where a driver is.

## The Demo Test: Watch What They Avoid

Every vendor demo is choreographed. That's fine — you'd do the same. What matters is what happens when you push past the script.

Ask to see a load with a problem on it. A detention claim that's been disputed. A settlement that needs a deduction for a fuel advance. A customer invoice that has to be corrected after it's already gone out. Good software handles the messy middle of freight, not just the clean happy path where every load picks up on time and every driver checks in on schedule. If the rep says "we'd have to set that up for a follow-up call," that's a signal the workflow doesn't exist yet, or exists but is clunky enough that they don't want you to see it live.

### The Squirm Questions

These are the ones that separate a vendor who respects your time from one who's hoping you sign before you notice the fine print:

- "Is this price per user, or is it a flat rate no matter how many dispatchers, safety staff, or drivers I add?"
- "If I cancel next quarter, what do I lose access to — my own load history and PODs, or just the software?"
- "Can I try this for real, with my own loads and my own drivers, before I put in a card number?"
- "Does your AI or automation feature actually rank loads by margin and deadhead, or is it a chatbot that answers FAQs?"
- "What's the actual uptime history, and where do I find it — not the marketing page, the status page?"

Watch the body language as much as the answer. A vendor that's proud of their pricing structure will tell you straight, fast, with numbers. A vendor that's built a business on per-seat upcharges will pivot to "it depends on your needs" and try to get you into a custom quote call. That call exists to find out how much you'll tolerate paying, not to find out what you need.

## Pricing Tricks Disguised As Flexibility

Per-user pricing is the oldest trick in TMS sales. It looks reasonable at ten trucks and turns into a real problem the moment you add a night dispatcher, a second safety hire, or a few owner-operators who need driver-facing access. Every seat is a new line item, and the vendor knows growth is exactly when you're least likely to renegotiate — you're busy running more freight.

## Run The Trial Like It's Your Busiest Week

A 30-day trial is only useful if you stress it. Don't run three test loads and call it evaluated. Load in your actual customer list, your actual driver roster, and a week of real freight. Cut a real settlement. Send a real invoice. Try the EDI tender against a shipper who actually sends you 204s. Pull a compliance report and see if it flags what your safety person already knows needs flagging. If the system holds up under your real volume — deadhead and detention and disputed accessorials included — you've learned something no demo could tell you.
